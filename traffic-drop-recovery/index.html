Organic traffic dropped. Here is how an expert finds out why.

A sudden fall in Google traffic feels like an emergency, and the instinct is to start changing things. Resist it. The first job is to work out which kind of drop you have, because the fix for a tracking error, a lost snippet, a Google update and a broken redirect have nothing in common.

Quick answer: why did my organic traffic drop?

Google's own troubleshooting guide groups traffic drops into six causes: technical problems, security issues, manual actions, algorithm updates, seasonality or changing search interest, and site moves or migrations. An expert diagnosis rules each one in or out using Search Console data, the timing of Google's announced updates and what changed on your site, so the fix targets the real cause instead of a guess.

The first 48 hours: what not to do

When the numbers fall, the temptation is to rewrite the homepage, buy links, change every title tag or rebuild the site. Each of those can make a bad week worse. If the cause was a measurement problem, you have changed a site that was fine. If it was a Google update, you have muddied the data you need to understand it. If it was a broken redirect, the new pages do nothing while the real fault keeps costing you.

Instead, freeze non-essential changes, write down everything that changed on or around the site in the last month (plugins, hosting, theme, redirects, content removals, a new tag manager setup), and pull the data before anyone touches it. That list is often where the answer is hiding.

Check this first: confirm the drop is real. Compare Google Search Console clicks with your analytics. If Search Console is steady but analytics fell, you most likely have a tracking or consent-banner problem, not an SEO problem.

The six causes Google lists, and how each one looks

Google Search Central publishes a guide to debugging search traffic drops. It is a good map, because it names the categories that cover nearly every case we see. Here is how each one tends to show up in the data.

1. Technical issues

Errors that stop Google crawling, indexing or serving pages. A site-wide outage, a stray noindex tag after a theme update, a robots.txt mistake, or a server that times out for Googlebot. Usually abrupt and often site-wide.

2. Security issues

Malware or hacked content can trigger browser warnings and a Security Issues notice in Search Console. Clicks collapse because people will not click through the warning.

3. Manual actions

A human reviewer found a spam-policy violation. You will see it in the Manual Actions report. Affected pages or the whole site lose visibility until the cause is fixed and a review is approved.

4. Algorithm updates

Core updates and smaller ranking changes. Timing lines up with the dates on Google's Search Status Dashboard, and the loss is spread across many queries rather than one broken page.

5. Seasonality and demand

Fewer people searching. Ski rentals in May, snow removal in July, tax help in June. Compare the same months last year, and check Google Trends for your main queries.

6. Site moves and migrations

New domain, new platform, changed URLs. Google says rankings can fluctuate while it recrawls; a medium-sized site may take a few weeks to be processed and larger sites longer. Missing redirects turn a dip into a long-term loss.

Reading the pattern in Search Console

The shape of the drop narrows the cause quickly. Google suggests widening the Performance report to the full 16 months first, so you can see whether the same dip happened last year, then comparing the drop period with a similar period before it.

What you seeWhat it usually points to
Clicks and impressions fall off a cliff on one day, across the whole siteTechnical fault, a manual action, a security issue or a migration problem
A gradual slide over several weeks that starts with an announced core updateAlgorithmic reassessment of content quality or usefulness
Impressions steady, clicks downLower click-through: a weaker title or snippet, a new AI Overview or other SERP feature above you, or competitors with richer results

Loss concentrated in one folder or templateA change to that template, internal links, or a section-level quality issue

Break the data down by page, query, country, device and search type (web, image, video, news). A drop that only affects image search, for example, has a completely different fix from one that hits your service pages.

Denver patterns worth knowing

A few drops show up again and again for Colorado businesses. Mountain and outdoor businesses swing hard with the seasons, so a fall after the end of ski season may be demand, not rankings. Contractors see searches shift with the weather: a dry spring changes landscaping demand, a hailstorm on the Front Range sends roofing searches spiking and then falling back. And businesses that serve both Denver and the mountain towns sometimes lose visibility in one area when a location page is consolidated or a service-area setting changes in their Business Profile.

None of these are mysterious once you look at the right comparison. They are only expensive when someone reacts to them as if they were a penalty.

What the expert diagnosis includes

With read-only access to Search Console and analytics, the diagnosis covers the full checklist and ends with a plain written finding.

  • Measurement check. Search Console versus analytics, consent banner and tag manager changes, filters and annotations.
  • Timeline overlay. Your drop plotted against Google's published update dates and your own change log.
  • Index and crawl review. Page indexing report, crawl stats, robots.txt, canonicals, noindex tags and server responses.
  • Query and page breakdown. Which pages and which searches lost clicks, and whether positions or click-through changed.
  • Manual action and security check. Both reports reviewed, plus a scan for injected spam pages.
  • Competitor and SERP review. What now ranks where you used to, and whether the results page itself changed.
  • Written finding and plan. The most likely cause, what was ruled out, and the fixes in order, with a fixed quote if you want us to carry them out.

What recovery realistically looks like

Recovery time depends entirely on the cause. Technical faults usually come back once the fault is fixed and Google recrawls the affected pages. Migration losses recover as redirects are repaired and signals consolidate, which can take weeks. Manual actions need the violation removed and a reconsideration request approved. Algorithmic drops are the slowest: Google says improvements may not be reflected until a later core update, and that there is no guarantee of a return to previous positions.

That last point matters. If anyone promises to restore your traffic by a date, ask how they know which cause you have. If they cannot tell you, they are selling activity, not a diagnosis.

Questions people ask

How do I know if my traffic drop is real or a tracking problem?

Compare Google Search Console with your analytics for the same dates. Search Console measures clicks from Google directly, so if it is steady while analytics fell, the problem is almost certainly measurement: a broken tag, a consent banner change or a filter. Fix tracking first, then look again.

Can a traffic drop be caused by Google adding AI Overviews?

It can reduce clicks even when your position has not changed. If impressions hold steady but clicks and click-through rate fall, check whether an AI Overview or another feature now appears above your result for those queries. That calls for a different response from a ranking loss.

How long does it take to recover from a traffic drop?

It depends on the cause. A technical fault can recover within weeks of being fixed. A migration problem recovers as redirects and signals are repaired. Google says drops from core updates may not recover until a later update, even after improvements, and that recovery is not guaranteed.

What access do you need to diagnose a drop?

Read-only access to Google Search Console and your analytics is enough to start, plus a list of what changed on the site recently. We do not need logins to your hosting or website for the diagnosis itself.

Should I stop all website changes while you investigate?

Pause non-essential changes, yes. New changes make it harder to see what caused the drop. Keep doing anything that is genuinely urgent, such as security fixes, and write down the date of every change.
