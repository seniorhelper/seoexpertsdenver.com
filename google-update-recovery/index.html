Recovering from a Google update, without guesswork

Google changes its ranking systems constantly and announces the bigger shifts. When one of those dates lines up with your losses, the question is not just what changed at Google. It is what Google's systems now think of your pages compared with the alternatives, and what you can credibly do about it.

Quick answer: how do you recover from a Google core update?

Google says a drop after a core update does not mean your site broke a rule, and that there may be nothing technical to fix. Recovery comes from making the affected content genuinely more useful than what now outranks it. Google also says improvements may not show until a later core update, and a return to previous rankings is not guaranteed. A spam update is different: there the fix is to remove whatever violates Google's spam policies.

Core updates and spam updates are not the same problem

Most "Google penalty" panic is really a core update. The difference decides everything about the response.

Core update

A broad reassessment

Google adjusts how its systems judge relevance and quality across the whole index. Nothing targets your site specifically. Pages that lose ground have not been punished; other pages are now judged more useful for those searches. Google describes core updates happening several times a year.

Spam update

An enforcement change

Google improves how its automated systems detect content or links that violate its spam policies, such as scaled content abuse, site reputation abuse, expired domain abuse or link spam. If you were caught, the fix is removal of the violating material, not better writing.

There are also smaller, unannounced changes going on all the time. Google notes that small ranking fluctuations happen continuously and can reverse on their own, which is one reason an expert looks for a sustained pattern before recommending changes.

Step one: confirm the update really is the cause

Plenty of drops coincide with an update by accident. Before anyone rewrites content, we check:

  • The dates. Google's Search Status Dashboard lists each announced update's start and end. Google suggests waiting at least a week after an update finishes before analysing, then comparing that week with the week before it began.
  • The size. A drop from position 2 to 4 calls for a different response from falling off page one. Google specifically warns against making drastic changes for small movements.
  • The scope. Site-wide, one section, or a handful of pages? A loss confined to one template often has a technical cause hiding behind the timing.
  • Other explanations. Seasonality, a site change the same week, a Manual Actions notice or a security issue all have to be ruled out first.

Step two: the honest content assessment

For a large, sustained drop, Google points site owners to its guidance on creating helpful, reliable, people-first content and suggests reviewing the most affected pages with those questions in mind, ideally with someone unaffiliated with the site. That is a key reason to bring in an outside expert: the owner and the person who wrote the pages are the least able to see their weaknesses.

The questions are uncomfortable on purpose. Does the page offer original information, research or analysis, or does it mostly restate what other sites say? Would a reader leave having learned enough to reach their goal? Does it show first-hand expertise? Was it written mainly to attract search visits rather than to serve the people who arrive?

We also look at what now ranks in your place. If the new winners answer the question faster, show real experience, or simply cover what searchers want, that is the gap to close. Sometimes the honest finding is that a page should be merged, rewritten from scratch, or removed.

What we do not do: swap words for synonyms, inflate word counts, add fake "expert reviewed" badges, or change publish dates to look fresh. Google's guidance specifically calls out cosmetic changes made to appear new, and none of it addresses why a page lost.

If it was a spam update

When the timing points to a spam update, the review turns to policy. Common findings on small business sites include bulk AI-written pages published without review, sections of a site rented out to third parties, purchased links or link exchanges, and doorway-style location pages that differ only by city name. The fix is to remove or substantially change the violating material. Google notes that improvements after a spam update may take months to be reflected, as its systems learn the site now complies.

If there is also a notice in Search Console's Manual Actions report, that is a separate process with a reconsideration request. See manual action removal.

Step three: a realistic recovery timeline

Google is unusually direct here. Content that was hit may not recover until the next core update, even after real improvements. Smaller changes between updates can help. Sometimes pages recover without the owner changing anything, and sometimes they do not recover at all because the competition improved. A responsible plan has milestones you can check, not a promised date.

Questions people ask

Was my site penalized by the core update?

Usually not in the sense people mean. Google says pages that drop in a core update have not been subjected to a manual or algorithmic action for spam. The systems now judge other pages more useful for those searches. Penalties for spam show up differently, and manual actions appear in Search Console.

How long after a core update can my site recover?

Google says content affected by a core update may not recover until the next core update rolls out, even after improvements, and that recovery is not guaranteed. Smaller updates in between can also help.

Should I delete pages that lost traffic?

Not automatically. Some pages should be improved, some merged with stronger pages and some removed. The decision depends on whether the page serves a real purpose for visitors. Deleting pages in a panic can remove content that was still earning links or conversions.

Can AI-written content cause a drop?

How content is produced is not the issue on its own. Google's spam policies target scaled content created mainly to manipulate rankings, whatever produces it. Pages that offer little original value, however they were written, tend to struggle in core updates.

Do you guarantee recovery from a Google update?

No, and no honest provider can. We guarantee a clear diagnosis, an outside assessment of the content and a plan you can measure.
