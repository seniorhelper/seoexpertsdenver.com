SEO for Colorado therapists and counseling practices

People looking for a therapist are often searching at a hard moment, and privately. A practice website has to be easy to find and reassuring to read, while handling sensitive information with real care. Some common marketing tools work against that.

Quick answer: how should a therapy practice approach SEO?

Make it easy for the right clients to find and trust you: clear pages for each specialty and population you serve, plain information about fees, insurance, telehealth and how to start, and an accurate Business Profile. Be careful with analytics and advertising pixels on pages about health conditions or appointment booking: federal guidance on online tracking by HIPAA-regulated providers means these tools need a deliberate decision, not a default install.

Privacy comes before marketing

The US Department of Health and Human Services Office for Civil Rights has published guidance on the use of online tracking technologies, such as analytics tags and advertising pixels, by organizations regulated under HIPAA. HHS notes that a federal court vacated part of that guidance in 2024, so the exact boundaries have shifted. Even so, the practical lesson for a private practice has not changed: tools that send visitors' data to third parties need careful thought on any page involving health conditions, booking or a client portal.

This is not legal advice, and your practice should take its own advice on HIPAA. From an SEO point of view, the good news is that you do not need invasive tracking to do SEO well. Google Search Console reports search clicks and queries without placing anything on your visitors' devices, and simple, privacy-conscious measurement of form submissions and calls is usually enough.

Check before you install. Website builders and booking tools often add analytics or advertising pixels by default. Find out what is running on your site, especially on intake and booking pages, and remove anything you have not decided to use.

How clients search for a therapist

Searches tend to combine a need with practical constraints, and both matter for which page should answer.

Pages that help clients decide

  • Specialty pages with substance. What working with you on that concern looks like, not a list of symptoms copied from elsewhere.
  • Credentials stated exactly. Colorado licenses are issued through the Department of Regulatory Agencies, which offers a public license lookup. Use the exact license type and link to verification if you like.
  • Fees and insurance. Session fees or ranges, insurance accepted or not, superbills and sliding scale policies. Uncertainty about cost stops many first calls.
  • Getting started. What the first contact and first session involve, how long until an appointment, and what to do in a crisis.
  • Crisis information. A clear note that the practice is not an emergency service, with the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline and local crisis options.

Write as you speak to clients: warm, direct and specific. That tone serves people at a difficult moment, and it also distinguishes your pages from generic health content.

Directories and your own site

Therapist directories often rank well for broad searches, and a good profile on the major ones is worthwhile. But they are rented space. Your own website is the only place you control fully, and it is where people go to check you out after finding you elsewhere. Keep your name, credentials, address or telehealth-only status and phone consistent everywhere.

For the Business Profile, a practice with an office where clients are seen can list it. A telehealth-only practice without a location clients visit generally is not eligible for a standard profile under Google's guidelines, so plan to rely more on your website and directories.

A note on reviews

Professional ethics codes for mental health professions generally discourage soliciting testimonials from current clients, because of the power dynamic in therapy. Check your own licensing board's rules and your professional association's code. Many practices simply do not ask for reviews, respond to existing ones without confirming anyone is a client, and build trust through content and credentials instead.

Group practices

Group practices face extra choices: one page per clinician, how to route specialty searches to the right person, and how to handle clinicians who join or leave. Pages for departing clinicians should be redirected thoughtfully rather than deleted, so searchers who look for them find the practice, not an error.

Questions people ask

Can I use Google Analytics on my therapy practice website?

Possibly, but it needs a deliberate decision. HHS has published guidance on tracking technologies used by HIPAA-regulated providers, part of which a court vacated in 2024. Get advice on your obligations, and be especially careful on pages about conditions, booking or client portals.

Can a telehealth-only practice have a Google Business Profile?

Generally not a standard profile, because Google's guidelines require a location where clients are served in person or a service area where you visit clients. Telehealth practices rely more on their website and directories.

Should therapists ask clients for Google reviews?

Many professional codes discourage soliciting testimonials from clients. Check your licensing board and professional association before asking.

Do directory profiles help my SEO?

They help people find you and can send referrals, and consistent listings support your overall presence. Your own site remains the most important place to explain how you work.
